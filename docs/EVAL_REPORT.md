### Notes and honest reading

- **Synthetic only.** Train, calibration and test shops all come from the same generator, so these figures say the pipeline is sound. They say nothing yet about real shops; the public-data method check (D-02) is still open.
- **History of this result (said plainly).** The first M-04 run used 200 shops (120 / 40 / 40). On its 40 test shops coverage was 94.0%, outside the 75% to 85% target: a **miss**. Repeating split, calibration and test over 30 random splits of the same 200 shops gave mean coverage 80.0% with a standard deviation of 7 points, and only 40% of splits landed inside the band, so 40 calibration plus 40 test shops were too few to measure coverage to within 5 points. The human then chose to grow the synthetic set to 1,000 shops (DEC-39). That change was made **after** seeing a test result. What did not change: the model settings, the leaf grid, the 80% conformal target, the method, the seed. Nothing was tuned on test shops. The test set here is the new 200-shop test split, read once.
- **Leaf size changed by itself.** With 600 training shops the shop-grouped CV (training shops only, DEC-36) now picks `min_child_samples` 20 instead of 60.
- **Tails are not symmetric.** The target is about 10% of months below P10 and 10% above P90. On the test shops it is closer to 6% below and 14% above: total coverage is on target, but actual sales land above P90 more often than below P10. A shop's 12 months move together, so the effective sample is 200 shops, not 2,400 months, and part of this may be chance; it is reported, not corrected.
- **Coverage is marginal, not per group.** Conformal calibration promises coverage on average. Small shops are over-covered and medium and large shops sit near the low edge of the target; tier 1 is lower than tier 3. Bands for larger shops are therefore slightly too narrow. Calibrating per size bucket would need a new decision.
- **Month 1 is the hardest month** (highest WAPE, lowest coverage): ramp-up speed is hidden from the model.
- **The baseline is a fair but simple one.** The peer median knows only tier, size bucket and month. The model also sees footfall drivers, competitors, rent and staff, which the generator uses to make revenue, so a large WAPE gain on synthetic data is expected and should not be read as real-world skill.

### Notes and honest reading

- **The target is met on synthetic shops**, at every history length, and for every k that was tried. This is the easy
  part: the set-up forecast knows only the shop's profile and location, the re-forecast has seen the shop's real sales.
- **The set-up forecast adds little once sales exist.** The shop's own model alone (ETS) is already far better than
  prior-only, and the blend at the chosen k is only slightly better than ETS alone at 1 and 2 months and equal at 3
  months. Cross-validation therefore picks a small k (10; ML_PLAN first proposed about 60). Synthetic daily sales are
  probably smoother than real kirana logs, so this k is likely too trusting of early sales for real shops. It must be
  re-tuned on pilot data (DEC-55).
- **The band formula was too narrow as first built.** With no multiplier, P10 to P90 covered about 65% of test months
  instead of 80%. A single multiplier, chosen on training shops only, brings coverage on test shops to 82% to 85%. The
  multiplier is large (3.7 at k = 10), which says the ETS residual spread understates the error of a forecast several
  months ahead. Treat the band as calibrated by experiment on synthetic shops, not as derived from theory.
- **The band does not get much narrower.** At the chosen setting the re-forecast band is 48% to 56% of P50 wide, against
  about 59% for the set-up forecast. The re-forecast improves the centre much more than it narrows the range. A larger k
  gives a narrower band at similar coverage but a worse centre (see the k table); the human chose the lower error.
- **The rescale by observed error often sits at its limit**: for more than a third of test cases it is at its lower
  limit (0.5), because many shops track their set-up forecast closely in the first months. The limits are assumptions.
- **Missing days:** accuracy falls gently (see the table) and stays far better than prior-only even with half the days
  missing. The band becomes too wide when days are missing (coverage well above 80%), because the multiplier was tuned on
  complete histories. That is the cautious direction, but it is a miscalibration and is reported as such.
- **Limits of this backtest:** origins only at month ends; only the next 3 months are scored; gaps are random single days
  (real gaps come in runs); tuning used a seeded sample of 300 of the 600 training shops; all shops come from one
  generator, so every number here is about the generator, not about real shops.

## ML4 stock advice (M-07)

**All shops are SYNTHETIC (`is_synthetic = true`).** The generator has no items: each category of a shop is simulated as one item whose demand is that category's daily sales in rupees (DEC-57). Every setting in `data/rules/stock.yaml` is an assumption.

- Rule: order when on hand + on order is at or below the reorder point (z = 1.28, lead time 2 days), quantity = 7 days of demand minus on hand and on order.
- Baseline: order the same 7 days of demand, but only when the shelf is empty (DEC-58).
- Both see only their own sales, start with the same stock, and the first 28 days are not scored.
- Demand estimate: days that ended with an empty shelf are left out (DEC-59).
- Half-life chosen on 200 calibration shops of the ML2 split (not test shops); results below are for 200 test shops.

### Target

Fewer stock-out days than the baseline, with average stock at most 1.25x the baseline (ML_PLAN section 8). **MET** on synthetic shops at the chosen half-life (7 days): item stock-out days per 30 days 4.05 against 9.57; average stock 0.95x the baseline. The rule had fewer stock-out days than the baseline for 100% of the test items.

### Notes and honest reading

- **The target was missed by the first version of the rule and is met only after a change to the demand estimate.**
  At first a day with an empty shelf counted as a day of low sales. The rule then cut stock-out days by about 65% but
  held 1.44x the baseline's average stock (limit 1.25x), mostly because the baseline's own estimate collapsed after each
  stock-out and it ordered too little. The human chose to leave empty-shelf days out of the estimate (DEC-59). The
  thresholds (z, lead time, cover) were not changed. Both variants are shown above.
- **The rule still runs out too often.** About 4 item-days in 30 end with an empty shelf and about 6% of demand is lost,
  which is worse than the roughly 90% service level that z = 1.28 suggests. Likely reasons: the weekly pattern and
  festival peaks are not in the estimate (a plain weighted mean), growing shops are under-estimated, and a day that ends
  at exactly zero counts as a stock-out day even if no sale was lost. This was not tuned away.
- **"Shop days with any item empty" is high for both policies** (about 14 against 22 in 30) because a shop has five
  simulated items and one empty item is enough. With real shops and many more items this number would be higher still;
  it is a strict measure.
- **The baseline is a simple strawman.** A real owner reorders before the shelf is completely empty. The gap to a real
  owner's habit is unknown and is not claimed here.
- **Limits:** one item per category with demand in rupees, not real items or units; a fixed 2-day lead time with no
  supplier delays, minimum order sizes or cash limits; the simulation knows what is on order, the app does not (it has no
  purchase orders), so in the app an item stays on the reorder list until the purchase is recorded; the category-average
  fallback for new items could not be tested in the simulation (one item per category) and is covered only by unit tests.
- The half-life choice (7 against 14 days) makes almost no difference on this data; 7 was kept by the stated rule.

### What was not evaluated

Low stock, dead stock, EMI due and data gap are exact rules with no statistics; they are covered by hand-calculated unit tests (`backend/tests/test_alerts.py`), not by this simulation.

### Notes and honest reading

- **Zero false alarms is a property of the synthetic data, not of the rule.** The generator has daily noise of about
  12%, festivals and a weekly pattern, but no closed days, strikes, bad-weather weeks or supplier problems. Real shops
  will produce false alarms; how many is unknown until pilot data exists. The figure must not be quoted as an expected
  rate.
- **The rule is blind to mild dips by design.** It catches about 1 in 5 dips of 25% to 30% and about 3 in 5 of 30% to
  35%. Dips of 35% to 45% are caught 89% of the time, below the 90% line on their own; the target is met because deeper
  dips are caught almost always. The band on which the target is judged was chosen by the human before the run (DEC-61).
- **Detection takes about 5 to 7 days** of low sales, because the 7-day average has to fall. A one-week dip that then
  recovers may never alert.
- **The generator's own dips** (29 test shops) are a small sample: 5 of 7 detected in the 35% to 45% band. About half
  of them are milder than 35%.
- **The injected dips are simple:** a sudden, flat cut of all categories. A slow slide over several weeks is not
  tested and would be caught late or not at all, because the 28-day base slides with it.
- **No tuning was done.** Thresholds are the ML_PLAN assumptions; the robust z definition and the minimum number of
  logged days (4 of 7 and 14 of 28) were fixed before the first run (DEC-63).
- With 20% of days missing the result barely changes; longer gaps (a whole missing week) switch the rule off, and the
  data-gap alert takes over.
- Not yet wired to the database: storing, de-duplicating and resolving alerts is B-08, the daily job is B-09. The EMI
  alert needs the owner's opening cash, which arrives with M-08; until then it stays silent (DEC-62).
